{% blocktrans trimmed %}The term "Law of Messiah" appears only once in the Bible, and that is in Galatians 6:2. That notwithstanding, the term is extremely significant in signaling that there exists such a law, that it is related to "Torah" and, as we shall see, that both it and "Torah" are alive and well under the New Covenant.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The Bible can be appreciated from many points of view. It may be thought of as the biography of God in His dealings with mankind. It may be read as a treatise on the history of the world and, in particular, the history of Israel and the heathen nations. It may also be studied as a prophetic book, revealing our future here on earth and in eternity. In this work, however, it is mainly presented as a book of Torah law, containing God's instructions for holy, moral, and victorious living.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}1. Torah and the Law{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The Theological Wordbook of the Old Testament defines "Torah" (Strong's number 8451) as follows 1 :{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The word tôrâ means basically "teaching" whether it is the wise man instructing his son or God instructing Israel. The wise give insight into all aspects of life so that the young may know how to conduct themselves and to live a long blessed life (Prov 3:1 f.). So too God, motivated by love, reveals to man basic insight into how to live with each other and how to approach God. Through the law God shows his interest in all aspects of man's life which is to be lived under his direction and care. Law of God stands parallel to word of the Lord to signify that law is the revelation of God's will (e.g. Isa 1:10). In this capacity it becomes the nation's wisdom and understanding so that others will marvel at the quality of Israel's distinctive life style (Deut 4:6). Thus there is a very similar understanding of the role of teaching with its results in the wisdom school, in the priestly instruction, and the role of the law with its results for all the people of the covenant.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Specifically law refers to any set of regulations; for example, Exodus 12 contains the law in regard to observing the Passover, and other specific laws include those for the various offerings, for leprosy, and for jealousy. In this light, law is often considered to consist of statutes, ordinances, precepts, commandments, and testimonies.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The meaning of the word gains further perspective in the light of Deuteronomy. There, Moses sets about to explain the law, and law includes moral, ceremonial, and interpretive dimensions. The book of Deuteronomy shows that the law has a broad meaning to encompass history, regulations and their interpretation, and exhortations. It is not merely the listing of casuistic statements as is the case in Hammurabi's code. Later the word extended to include the first five books of the Bible in all their variety.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}In addition, The New Brown-Driver-Briggs-Gesenius Hebrew and English Lexicon gives "direction, instruction, law" as the primary definitions of "Torah" 2 , and the Encyclopaedia Judaica states: 3{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Torah is derived from the root which in the hifil conjugation means "to teach" (cf. Lev. 10:11). The meaning of the word is therefore "teaching", "doctrine", or "instructions"; the commonly accepted "law" gives a wrong impression. The word is used in different ways but the underlying idea of "teaching" is common to all.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}There are basically two ways in which "Torah" is used in the Tanakh. In one usage, "Torah" refers to "the Law" - God's legal code which provides instructions on specific matters. Examples of this usage are: 4{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}"This is the Torah of the burnt offering..." (Leviticus 6:2(9)ff); "grain offering..." (Leviticus 6:7(14)ff); "sin offering..." (Leviticus 6:18(25)ff); "trespass offering..." (Leviticus 7:1ff); "peace offering..." (Leviticus 7:11ff); "leprous plague..." (Leviticus 13:59ff); "jealousy..." (Numbers 5:29ff).{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Where "Torah" means "law", it is usually accompanied by other Hebrew words having to do with law, such as:{% endblocktrans %}

  • {% blocktrans trimmed %}mitzvah (commandment - Strong's 4687) 5{% endblocktrans %}
  • {% blocktrans trimmed %}khukah (statue or ordinance ("regulation" in CJB) - Strong's 2708) 6{% endblocktrans %}
  • {% blocktrans trimmed %}mishpat (judgement ("ruling" in CJB) - Strong's 4941) 7{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The following examples contain the word "Torah" in addition to one or more of the above words, showing that "Torah" is connected to, but distinguishable from, commandments, statutes, ordinances and judgments:{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Numbers 19:2: This is the regulation from the Torah which Adonai has commanded. Here, "Torah" cannot mean "regulation" or "ordinance".{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Numbers 31:21: ... This is the regulation from the Torah which Adonai has ordered Moshe. Here also, "Torah" cannot mean "regulation" or "ordinance".{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Deuteronomy 30:10: ... so that you obey his mitzvot and regulations which are written in this book of the Torah ... Some translations of this verse speak of "statutes" rather than "regulations", so here, "Torah" cannot mean "mitzvot (commandments)" or "regulations (statutes)".{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Psalm 89:31-32(30-31): If his descendants abandon my Torah and fail to live by my rulings, if they profane my regulations and don't obey my mitzvot ... Here, "Torah" cannot mean "rulings (judgments)", "regulations (statutes)" or "mitzvot (commandments)".{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Another way of distinguishing "Torah" (teaching) from commandment, statute, ordinance, and judgment is by its context. In Scripture, the word can clearly mean teaching when it appears in passages such as Exodus 13:9, Exodus 16:4, Psalm 1:2, Psalm 37:31, Psalm 40:9, Proverbs 6:23, Proverbs 7:2, and Proverbs 13:14.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}2. Torah in the New Covenant{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The New Covenant was prophesied in Jeremiah 31:30–33 8 :{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}'Here the days are coming,' says Adonai, 'when I will make a new covenant with the house of Isra'el and with the house of Y'hudah. It will not be like the covenant I made with their fathers on the day I took them by their hand and brought them out of the land of Egypt; because they, for their part, violated my covenant, even though I, for my part, was a husband to them,' says Adonai. 'For this is the covenant I will make with the house of Isra'el after those days,' says Adonai: 'I will put my Torah within them and write it on their hearts; I will be their God, and they will be my people.'{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Jeremiah is quoting God as saying "I will put My Torah in their minds, and write it on their hearts." This Scripture is repeated in the Greek New Covenant Scriptures in Hebrews 8:8–12 9 . Since the "better covenant" in Hebrews 8:6 is the "New Covenant" which is referred to in Jeremiah 31:31 and Hebrews 8:8, one must conclude that the New Covenant was given as Torah, and therefore is Torah. 10{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Since in the Hebrew text of this Scripture the word for "law" is Torah, the passage can also be read as a statement that the New Covenant is itself Torah. That same connection is reflected in the Greek New Covenant Scriptures through nomos, which can be used for Torah in the sense of God's revealed teaching and law. 11{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}A related Greek word, nomotheteo, occurs in Hebrews 8:6 and is translated "established" in some English versions. David H. Stern renders the verse as "For this covenant has been given as Torah on the basis of better promises," reinforcing the connection between the New Covenant and Torah. 12{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}3. What Makes the Pentateuch Torah?{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Since "Torah" is God's teaching, there is a sense in which all Scripture is "Torah." Nevertheless, Yeshua himself recognized a distinction between the "Torah" of the Pentateuch and other classifications of Scriptures (Luke 24:44) 13 .{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The uniqueness of the Pentateuch is that it contains God's major covenants prior to Yeshua. The other books of the Tanakh expound on these covenants, teach about them, prophesy about them, and present their history, but they do not themselves contain the covenants.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}All Scripture is God-breathed and valuable for teaching the truth, convicting of sin, correcting faults, and training in right living, so there is still a sense in which the whole Bible functions as Torah. The Pentateuch is unique, however, in that it contains the covenantal foundation on which the rest of the Tanakh builds.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}4. The Gospels as Torah{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The New Covenant is a covenant of Torah — God's teaching written on our hearts. If the test for "pentateuchal" Torah is whether a New Covenant book of the Bible contains the New Covenant itself, then the Gospel books — Matthew, Mark, Luke and John certainly pass the test. Among the many parallels between the Pentateuch and the Gospels:{% endblocktrans %}

  1. {% blocktrans trimmed %}The Pentateuch contains the life of Moses, who was used by God to deliver the Sinai Covenant 14 . The Gospels contain the life of Yeshua, who was used by God to deliver the New Covenant.{% endblocktrans %}
  2. {% blocktrans trimmed %}The Pentateuch contains the event in which Moses proclaims the blood of the Sinai Covenant. The Gospels contain the event in which Yeshua proclaims his blood of the New Covenant.{% endblocktrans %}
  3. {% blocktrans trimmed %}The Pentateuch contains the teachings and commandments of the Sinai Covenant. The Gospels contain the teachings and commandments of the New Covenant.{% endblocktrans %}
  4. {% blocktrans trimmed %}The Pentateuch contains the means by which men must atone for their sins through animal sacrifice. The Gospels contain the means by which men must be forgiven for their sins through Yeshua's sacrifice.{% endblocktrans %}
  5. {% blocktrans trimmed %}The Pentateuch initiates a priesthood and the appointment of Aaron as High Priest. The Gospels initiate a new priesthood and the appointment of Yeshua as High Priest 15 .{% endblocktrans %}
  6. {% blocktrans trimmed %}The Pentateuch contains shadows of things to come. The Gospels contain the prophetic fulfillment of those shadows.{% endblocktrans %}
  7. {% blocktrans trimmed %}The Pentateuch ends with the death of Moses. The Gospels end with the death, resurrection and ascension of Yeshua.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Yeshua's life and blood are the substance of the New Covenant, and his sacrificial death and resurrection mark both the New Covenant's beginning and its fulfillment. It is in the Gospel books that we find this New Covenant substance, and therefore the New Covenant itself.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}5. The Mosaic Law: Rabbinical View{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}According to Rabbinical understanding, the Holy Scriptures are organized into three parts — the Torah (Pentateuch, Law), the Nevi'im (Prophets), and the Ketuvim (Holy Writings) 16 . Rabbinical Judaism does not acknowledge the current existence of the "New Covenant" prophesied in Jeremiah 31:31, or the inspiration of the New Covenant Scriptures. One of the ways that Judaism has historically viewed "Torah" has been as synonymous with the Law of Moses — the law to which it considers itself bound today.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Its further understanding is that all of the Mosaic Law is contained in the Torah, and that the remaining Scriptures of the Tanakh, while inspired, merely historicize, exemplify, and embellish. Consequently, Rabbinical Judaism devotes most of its attention to the Torah and to the oral tradition, to which it looks for interpretation.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}6. Statutes of the Mosaic Law: Dependent and Transcendent{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The Commandments contained in the Tanakh are God's explicit directives and are, therefore, divine statutory law 17 . There are two categories of statutes commanded by God under the Mosaic Covenant — those whose literal compliance depends upon the Covenant's continued existence ("covenant-dependent"), and those whose literal compliance does not ("covenant-transcendent").{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Hebrews 8:13 suggests a diminishing literal role for covenant-dependent statutes; consequently, such statutes are not incorporated into New Covenant law in their original form and application, while statutes that are covenant-transcendent are readily adopted without change 18 . A rule-of-thumb test is to ask: "Can or should this statute be complied with in the New Covenant era exactly and literally as commanded?" If the answer is "yes", it is "transcendent". If "no", it is "dependent".{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}It is my opinion that "oldness of the letter", as some translations read, refers to the diminishing role of covenant-dependent statutes as literal law, whereas our need for literal obedience to covenant-transcendent statutes is timeless. In practice, the distinction is not always simple, so judgment must be exercised carefully in deciding whether a command should be carried over literally or only in principle 19 .{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}7. Torah Underlies the Statutes{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Underlying each of God's commandments is Torah, which is broadly defined as His essential teaching 21 22 . Although not all Torah is statutory in origin, all Torah is God's law because it is the essence of His will. Even if a commandment is covenant-dependent, its Torah transcends the change in covenant because God's values never change.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Torah is not only revealed through Mosaic commandments, but through all Scripture 23 . Thus, not only does Torah pass into the New Covenant, it defines it, and without it there would be no New Covenant 24 25 26 .{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}8. The Law of Messiah{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}In Galatians 6:2, Paul instructs us:{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Bear one another's burdens — in this way you will be fulfilling the Torah's true meaning, which the Messiah upholds.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Most English translations of this verse employ the expression "the Law of Messiah" 27 , which is from where the title of this book is derived 28 . Although this verse is the only occurrence of the term in Scripture, the Apostolic writings are replete with references to operative law in the New Covenant.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}We have seen that there are two components of Law in the Tanakh that are eternal: (1) covenant-transcendent statutes, and (2) Torah (teaching) that is revealed through all Scripture. Since covenant-dependent statutes vanish along with their vanishing covenant, it is the covenant-transcendent statutes, plus all Torah contained in the Tanakh, plus all law added by the New Covenant Scriptures, which form the "Law of Messiah" to which we are accountable today. Hence, the Law of Messiah is the New Covenant counterpart of the Law of Moses.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Examples of operative New Covenant law include the instructions of Yeshua in the Gospels, the commands of the Apostolic writings, and the apostolic recognition that God's law is written on the hearts of Gentiles who naturally do what the Torah requires.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}9. Dibeyr, Torah and Mitzvot{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}As used in this book, a "dibeyr" is an explicit commandment of God in Scripture — an imperative directive (usually a statute or ordinance of the Mosaic Law) which may be either covenant-dependent or transcendent. "Torah" can mean several things but, in the absence of the article "the", it most often means God's essential and eternal teaching or instruction. Torah underlies all of God's dibrot 29 , and is revealed (explicitly or implicitly) by all Scripture — not just the Scriptures of the Pentateuch. A "Mitzvah" is an interpreted statement of God's will, derived from Scripture, and expressed in the form of a commandment.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}There have been several attempts in history to codify God's Word into numbered mitzvot 30 . The earliest was Hilchot Gedolot, published in the 8th century. By that time, a principle had already been established in the Talmud that the total number of mitzvot in the Torah was Taryag (613) 31 .{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}10. Maimonides and Yisrael Meir haKohen{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Moshe ben Maimon (Maimonides, the "RAMBAM"), sometime prior to 1170 C.E., wrote his compilation of Torah law under the title Kitab Al-Fara'id (The Book of Divine Precepts) 34 . Unlike his predecessors, Maimonides was careful to follow defined principles (fourteen) to justify his conclusions. His work received almost universal acceptance.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}In 1931, Rabbi Yisrael Meir haKohen published Sefer haMitzvot haKatzar (The Concise Book of Mitzvoth) 36 , in which he extracted from Maimonides' list 297 mitzvot — 77 positive, 194 negative, and 26 applicable only in the Land of Israel 37 . Rabbi Meir intended his book to be a compilation of mitzvot that could be observed by Jews in the post-Temple era and, particularly in the Diaspora.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Maimonides and Meir do not always agree on the statement of the mitzvah that they extract from a given Scripture, and their translations and editorial apparatus also differ in chapter and verse citation 38 39 40 41 . Those differences matter because this book is not merely reproducing lists, but attempting to interpret the underlying scriptural intent.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}11. Other Classical Enumerations{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Codifications written after Sefer haMitzvot have added to our overall understanding of God's commandments, but no other codifier ever attained the prestige and influence of Maimonides 42 . Several deserve special mention:{% endblocktrans %}

  • {% blocktrans trimmed %}Mishneh Torah , a fourteen-book compilation of laws gleaned from both Scripture and Talmud by Maimonides.{% endblocktrans %}
  • {% blocktrans trimmed %}Sefer Mitzvot Gadol (Big Book of Commandments), Moses of Coucy (13th century).{% endblocktrans %}
  • {% blocktrans trimmed %}Sefer Mitzvot Katan (Little Book of Commandments), Isaac ben Joseph of Corveil (13th century).{% endblocktrans %}
  • {% blocktrans trimmed %}Sefer HaChinuch (The Book of Education), attributed to Aaron haLevi of Barcelona (c. 1257), still in common use today 43 44 .{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Shulchan Aruch and Kitzur Shulchan Aruch are beyond the scope of this book because they combine Scriptural and rabbinical elements. The present work is narrower in aim: to stay focused on Scripture-based mitzvot and their New Covenant application.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}12. Need for a New Covenant Codification{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}For Messianic Jews and Torah-adherent Gentile believers (followers of Messiah Yeshua), neither Maimonides', nor Meir's, nor HaChinuch's works are a sufficient guide for daily conduct and for relating to God 45 . It is because these believers acknowledge the continuing priesthood of Yeshua, look to regular communication with the Ruach HaKodesh for personal guidance 46 , and seek to be obedient to additional Torah contained in the Kitvey B'rit Chadasha (the New Testament Scriptures) 47 - all part of a New Covenant between God and the Jewish people 48 .{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}New Covenant believers (both Jews and Gentiles) have the same need as Rabbinical Jews for a handy compilation of Scripture-based mitzvot to assist them in their walk of obedience. The challenge in writing this book was to provide such a tool — one that parallels the older works in form, but which adds additional Scriptures and provides interpretation for New Covenant usage.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Underlying this attempt is the conviction that the Torah God gave to the ancient Israelites is relevant today for both the Jew and the non-Jew, albeit sometimes differently for each.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Exodus 12:49 says, "The same teaching is to apply equally to the citizen and to the foreigner living among you." Romans 15:4 says that everything written in the past was written to teach us, so that with the encouragement of the Tanakh we might patiently hold on to our hope. First Corinthians 7:19 says that being circumcised means nothing, and being uncircumcised means nothing; what does mean something is keeping God's commandments. Galatians 3:24 says that the Torah functioned as a custodian until the Messiah came. Second Timothy 3:16-17 says that all Scripture is God-breathed and is valuable for teaching the truth, convicting of sin, correcting faults and training in right living. Hebrews 10:1 says that the Torah has in it a shadow of the good things to come, but not the actual manifestation of the originals.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}This verse of Scripture is sometimes wrongly generalized to mean that all the commandments of Torah are applicable to Jews and Gentiles in the same way. That is a mistaken view because the context of Exodus 12:49 is of the sojourner who desired to eat the Pesach and thus needed to be circumcised 49 . Even if one generalizes the verse from its narrow context of Pesach and circumcision, the CJB's choice of the word "teaching" rather than "law" suggests that there may be different applications for the Jew and the Gentile 50 .{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}13. Considerations in Writing this Book{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The matter of proper application of Scripture looms very large. The author approached this work prayerfully, beginning with the mitzvot promulgated by Rabbi Yisrael Meir HaKohen in his Sefer haMitzvot haKatzar 51 , determined which mitzvot in Maimonides' 52 , 53 and HaChinuch's 54 compilations corresponded, and then compared each with the Scriptures they were intended to interpret. As a final step, New Covenant Scriptures that appeared relevant were consulted 55 . 56{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Not being bound by Talmudic authority, the author did not attempt to arrive at Taryag (613) mitzvot and did not follow Maimonides' practice of listing mitzvot in categories of positive and negative. Mitzvot that addressed the same or similar issues were combined. All of this was done in collaboration with Dr. Daniel C. Juster whose comments are liberally included.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Differences in the chapter and verse citations among the various translated sources can be explained by the fact that the translators and editors, not Maimonides, Meir, or HaChinuch, placed them there. After merging the work of Meir and Maimonides and eliminating duplications, the resulting list was reduced by eliminating those mitzvot that were judged to be not literally observable in the New Covenant 57 .{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}For example, Meir's Positive Mitzvah #MP52 (Deuteronomy 18:4) requires that we give the kohen the first wool of our sheep. With the Mosaic Covenant in the process of vanishing away or having already vanished, there is no longer a reason to support the Levitical kohanim with such gifts 58 59 . Although the New Covenant has rendered some of the Mosaic mitzvot literally unobservable, it also produced new ones, and changed how some of the Mosaic mitzvot are to be applied today. Consider, for example, Meir's Positive Mitzvah #MP38 (Leviticus 25:35-36 & Deuteronomy 15:8), in which he states that we are to give charity to the poor in Jewry. Under the New Covenant, however, our brethren include non-Jews of the faith grafted to Israel as well, and so the mitzvah is broadened to include our poor brethren who dwell among us 60 .{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}14. Matters Left for a Future Volume{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Although New Covenant Scriptures (especially mitzvot spoken by Yeshua) are liberally referenced in this work, a methodical list and commentary of all the mitzvot in the New Testament remains for a future volume. As a foretaste, an Appendix listing the mitzvot that appear in the Book of Matthew is included, as well as an Appendix listing mitzvot spoken by the shlichim in the New Testament.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}As a foretaste of what is planned to come, this introduction points toward a larger structure that includes appendices and indexes rather than trying to do everything here.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}There is a modern work, "The Mitzvot: The Commandments and their Rationale" by Abraham Chill 61 , that I consult from time to time and that deserves special mention because, in addition to his own commentary, he includes extensive references to classical rabbinical literature.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}There is another modern work "The 613 Mitzvot: A Contemporary Guide to the Commandments of Judaism" by Ronald L. Eisenberg 62 , that I recommend for its clarity of commentary, and its excellent indices that allow one to easily determine Maimonides' mitzvah number from its Scripture citation, and vice versa.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}In addition to the rabbinical commentaries that focus on the mitzvot, there are others of a more general character, and there are also Messianic Jewish commentaries such as David H. Stern's Jewish New Testament Commentary 63 , which I consult from time to time.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Finally, I should like to say a word about usage. The writings of Meir, Maimonides, HaChinuch, and my writings as well, are attempts at interpretation, codification, explanation, and application of mitzvot that we believe we have found in the Scriptures. Although we did not write or compose new commandments, this book follows the common usage of saying that XYZ "wrote" mitzvot, or of referring to one of our writings as "XYZ's mitzvah" when talking about our compilations and explanations. I follow this convention of usage both for the historical commentators as well as for myself within this book and by doing so, no disrespect to God is intended.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}16. Two Kinds of Mitzvot{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The Law of Messiah Mitzvot in this book are of two kinds: (1) inclusive of one or more of the classical mitzvot (those from Maimonides, Meir, or HaChinuch), or (2) completely unrelated to the classical mitzvot. The classical mitzvot are based only upon Scriptures from the Torah, whereas the Mitzvot written by the authors herein are based upon Scriptures throughout the Bible.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}Those containing Scripture references from the classical mitzvah compilations are marked by an asterisk in the Table of Contents, and one or more of the referenced Scriptures carry the label "Key Scriptures". In the Mitzvot that are unrelated, their "Key Scriptures" are those that appear to the authors as most relevant; these are not necessarily presented in a canonical order.{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}17. Choice of Translation{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The mitzvot which are the subject of this book derive from the original biblical languages and not from any English or other translation. Nevertheless, because this book was written with the English speaker in mind, it was necessary that I select an English translation in which the Scriptures being discussed could be displayed. I chose to use David H. Stern's 1998 translation known as the Complete Jewish Bible (CJB) 64 because, more than any other, it brings out the Jewish background and nuances of the Scriptures, and this despite Dr. Stern himself stating:{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}So the Tanakh you have in this book is something between a translation and a paraphrase; since it is partly one and partly the other, I refuse to define it as either and instead call it simply a "version". On the other hand, the books of the New Covenant are my translation from the original Greek. 65{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}19. A Work to Build Upon{% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %}The uniqueness of this book is not so much in its commentary (although I have prayerfully sought God's wisdom while writing it), but in its utility as a handbook and a framework for others to build upon. I am aware that there are some who might criticize me for publishing my personal views and interpretations of God's commandments, preferring that a work such as this be undertaken by a plurality of scholars and with the authority of a community behind it. In my defense, I will only say that this work is no different than any other commentary on Scripture, most of which have been written by individuals. Indeed, Maimonides himself came under criticism for not citing Talmudic sources to support his application of rules for deciding which mitzvot to codify 66 , and for not disclosing the identity of the scholars that declared the rules on which he relied 67 .{% endblocktrans %}
